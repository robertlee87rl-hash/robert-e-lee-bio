Robert E. Lee (1807–1870)

A short biography and overview

Biography content

Robert Edward Lee was born on January 19, 1807, in Stratford Hall, Virginia, into one of the state's most prominent families. He graduated second in his class from the United States Military Academy at West Point in 1829 without receiving a single demerit, and went on to serve with distinction in the U.S. Army for over three decades. During the Mexican-American War, Lee earned recognition for his tactical brilliance and bravery, working closely with General Winfield Scott. He later served as superintendent of West Point and commanded forces that suppressed John Brown's raid at Harpers Ferry in 1859.

When the Civil War began in 1861, Lee faced a profound personal dilemma. Though he opposed secession and found slavery morally troubling, he declined command of Union forces and instead chose to defend his home state of Virginia after it joined the Confederacy. As commander of the Army of Northern Virginia, Lee became the most celebrated Confederate general, known for his audacious tactics and his victories at battles including Second Bull Run, Fredericksburg, and Chancellorsville. Despite being frequently outnumbered and under-supplied, his military acumen made him a formidable opponent until his surrender to Ulysses S. Grant at Appomattox Court House in April 1865.

After the war, Lee worked toward reconciliation between North and South, discouraging the construction of Confederate monuments and advocating for reunification. He became president of Washington College (now Washington and Lee University) in Lexington, Virginia, where he focused on education and rebuilding. Lee transformed the struggling institution into a respected university while encouraging his students to become productive citizens of a reunited nation. He died on October 12, 1870, leaving a complex legacy as both a skilled military commander and a central figure in one of America's most divisive conflicts. His life continues to be studied and debated as historians grapple with his role in defending the Confederacy and the institution of slavery.
